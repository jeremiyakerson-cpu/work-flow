# Domain 2 — Environmental & Toxicological Emergencies (topics 0-2)

@ 2.0
? A 24-year-old arrives 40 minutes after swallowing about thirty amitriptyline tablets. She is awake, talking and protecting her airway. Which decontamination measure should the nurse anticipate?
+ A single dose of activated charcoal by mouth
- Syrup of ipecac to induce vomiting | Ipecac is no longer recommended and risks aspiration if she deteriorates.
- Whole bowel irrigation by nasogastric tube | Irrigation is reserved for sustained-release drugs, metals and packets.
- Observation only until symptoms appear | Early charcoal can limit absorption of a potentially lethal dose.
= Activated charcoal (about 1 g/kg) is most useful within roughly an hour of a serious ingestion in an alert patient with an intact airway.

? Which patient should NOT receive activated charcoal?
+ A drowsy patient who swallowed drain cleaner
- An alert patient who took carbamazepine 30 minutes ago | An alert patient with a recent adsorbable ingestion is a candidate.
- An alert patient who took aspirin an hour ago | Salicylates are adsorbed and the airway is intact.
- An intubated patient who took a tricyclic antidepressant | A protected airway makes charcoal acceptable.
= Charcoal is contraindicated with caustics, where it obscures endoscopy and adds no benefit, and with an unprotected airway.

? A toddler swallowed a handful of prenatal vitamins containing iron. Why will activated charcoal not be ordered?
+ Iron does not bind to charcoal
- Charcoal is never given to children | Charcoal can be used in children when indicated.
- Charcoal turns iron into a toxic compound | No such reaction occurs.
- Iron is absorbed too slowly to matter | Iron is absorbed readily and is highly toxic.
= Metals such as iron and lithium, alcohols and caustics are poorly adsorbed by activated charcoal.

? A parent calls the triage line: a 2-year-old swallowed some of a grandparent's pills 10 minutes ago and is acting normally. The parent asks whether to make the child vomit. What is the best advice?
+ Do not induce vomiting; call poison control and bring the pill bottles
- Give syrup of ipecac and observe at home | Ipecac is obsolete and delays effective care.
- Give a glass of milk and put the child down for a nap to see what happens | Sleep hides deterioration and milk is not an antidote.
- Stimulate the gag reflex with a finger | Forced vomiting risks aspiration and removes little drug.
= Induced emesis is not recommended; poison control (1-800-222-1222) guides management based on the substance and amount.

? A man suspected of swallowing packets of cocaine is asymptomatic. Which decontamination method is most appropriate?
+ Whole bowel irrigation with polyethylene glycol solution
- Gastric lavage with a large-bore tube placed through the mouth | Lavage may rupture packets and does not clear the intestine.
- Repeated doses of syrup of ipecac | Vomiting risks packet rupture and aspiration.
- A sodium phosphate enema | An enema does not clear the small bowel and risks electrolyte shifts.
= Whole bowel irrigation flushes intact drug packets, sustained-release tablets, iron and lithium through the gut.

? A 19-year-old is agitated and picking at the air. Skin is hot, dry and flushed, pupils are 7 mm, HR 132, and the bladder is distended. Which ingestion is most likely?
+ Diphenhydramine
- Oxycodone | Opioids cause miosis, sedation and respiratory depression.
- An organophosphate insecticide | Cholinergic poisoning causes secretions, miosis and bradycardia.
- Clonidine | Clonidine causes miosis, bradycardia and hypotension.
= Hot, dry, flushed skin with mydriasis, tachycardia, delirium and urinary retention is the anticholinergic toxidrome.

? A farm worker has profuse salivation, tearing, vomiting, diarrhea, wheezing, pinpoint pupils and HR 44 after spraying crops. What is the first nursing action?
+ Don protective equipment and remove his clothing
- Start an IV line and draw baseline labs | Access matters, but staff protection and decontamination come first.
- Give activated charcoal by mouth | The exposure is dermal and inhalational, and he is vomiting.
- Place him in a negative-pressure room | Airborne isolation does not address chemical contamination.
= Organophosphates are absorbed through skin; staff protection and decontamination precede treatment to stop ongoing exposure.

? An unresponsive 30-year-old has a respiratory rate of 6, pinpoint pupils and SpO2 84%. Which toxidrome does this represent?
+ Opioid
- Sympathomimetic | Stimulants cause agitation, mydriasis and tachycardia.
- Anticholinergic | That syndrome causes mydriasis, delirium and dry skin.
- Serotonin syndrome | That causes clonus, hyperthermia and agitation.
= Coma, respiratory depression and miosis form the classic opioid triad.

? Two agitated, tachycardic, hyperthermic patients have dilated pupils. Which finding best separates sympathomimetic poisoning from anticholinergic poisoning?
+ Profuse sweating
- An elevated heart rate | Tachycardia occurs in both syndromes.
- Dilated pupils | Mydriasis occurs in both syndromes.
- Agitation | Altered behaviour occurs in both syndromes.
= Sympathomimetics cause diaphoresis, whereas anticholinergics block sweating and leave the skin dry.

? A 3-year-old drank a mouthful of lamp oil and is coughing. SpO2 is 95%. Which intervention is appropriate?
+ Observe respiratory status and avoid inducing vomiting
- Insert a nasogastric tube and lavage | Lavage raises the risk of aspirating the hydrocarbon.
- Give activated charcoal | Hydrocarbons are poorly adsorbed and charcoal invites vomiting.
- Discharge at once since the amount was small | Even small aspirations cause delayed pneumonitis.
= Hydrocarbons have low viscosity and are easily aspirated, causing chemical pneumonitis; emesis and lavage are avoided.

? An overdose patient's identity and ingestion are unknown. Which bedside test should be performed first in any patient with altered mental status?
+ A capillary blood glucose
- A urine drug screen | The screen is slow, incomplete and rarely changes immediate care.
- A serum ethanol level | Alcohol level does not exclude other reversible causes.
- An ammonia level | Ammonia is not a first-line test in an unknown ingestion.
= Hypoglycemia is rapidly reversible and mimics intoxication, so glucose is checked immediately in altered mental status.

@ 2.1
? A 52-year-old on long-term oxycodone for cancer pain is found with a respiratory rate of 7 after an accidental double dose. Which naloxone approach is best?
+ Small titrated IV doses until respirations are adequate
- A 2 mg IV bolus repeated until fully awake | Full reversal precipitates severe withdrawal and uncontrolled pain.
- A 10 mg IV bolus given once | That dose far exceeds what is needed and provokes withdrawal.
- No naloxone at all, because he is opioid dependent and in pain | Dependence does not preclude treating respiratory depression.
= In opioid-dependent patients naloxone is titrated in small increments to restore ventilation without precipitating withdrawal.

? A patient who overdosed on methadone wakes fully after naloxone and wants to leave 30 minutes later. What should the nurse explain?
+ Naloxone wears off before methadone, so breathing may slow again
- Naloxone has cured the overdose | The opioid outlasts the antagonist.
- He can leave once he has eaten | Eating does not change opioid duration.
- A second dose given just before leaving makes it safe for him to go | Repeat dosing still needs monitoring.
= Naloxone lasts 30-90 minutes while methadone lasts many hours, so prolonged observation or an infusion is needed.

? A 45-year-old who takes clonazepam daily for a seizure disorder overdoses on it along with amitriptyline. A provider orders flumazenil. What is the nurse's best action?
+ Question the order because of the risk of seizures
- Give it rapidly to prevent respiratory arrest | Rapid reversal may trigger refractory seizures here.
- Give it with a dose of naloxone | Adding naloxone does not reduce the seizure risk.
- Give half the dose intramuscularly | The route change does not remove the danger.
= Flumazenil can precipitate withdrawal seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients and unmask seizures from proconvulsant co-ingestants.

? A patient who overdosed on a tricyclic antidepressant has HR 128, BP 84/50 and a QRS duration of 140 ms. Which medication should the nurse prepare?
+ Sodium bicarbonate IV
- Physostigmine IV | Physostigmine can cause asystole in tricyclic poisoning.
- Procainamide IV | Sodium channel blockers worsen the conduction delay.
- Flumazenil IV | Flumazenil does not treat sodium channel blockade.
= Sodium bicarbonate overcomes sodium channel blockade and narrows the QRS in tricyclic toxicity.

? A patient who took an overdose of metoprolol has HR 38 and BP 76/40 that does not respond to atropine and fluids. Which antidote should the nurse anticipate?
+ Glucagon IV
- Naloxone IV | Naloxone reverses opioids only.
- Flumazenil IV | Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines only.
- Vitamin K IV | Vitamin K reverses warfarin only.
= Glucagon raises cyclic AMP independently of beta receptors, improving rate and contractility; high-dose insulin is also used.

? A patient with a verapamil overdose remains hypotensive and bradycardic after IV calcium. Which therapy is recommended next?
+ High-dose insulin with dextrose
- Oral potassium chloride | Potassium does not treat calcium channel blockade.
- IV digoxin | Digoxin would worsen conduction block.
- IV magnesium sulfate | Magnesium further blocks calcium effects.
= High-dose insulin euglycemic therapy improves myocardial energy use and contractility in calcium channel blocker poisoning.

? An 80-year-old on digoxin presents with vomiting, confusion, potassium 6.4 mEq/L and runs of ventricular tachycardia. Which treatment is definitive?
+ Digoxin immune Fab
- Hemodialysis | Digoxin's large volume of distribution makes dialysis ineffective.
- Synchronized cardioversion | Electrical therapy may trigger refractory ventricular fibrillation.
- IV furosemide | Diuresis does not clear digoxin and may lower potassium unpredictably.
= Life-threatening dysrhythmias or hyperkalemia from digoxin toxicity are treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments.

? A man who drank windshield washer fluid has blurred "snowfield" vision, an anion gap of 28 and an elevated osmolar gap. Which antidote should the nurse prepare?
+ Fomepizole
- N-acetylcysteine | That is the antidote for acetaminophen.
- Pyridoxine alone | Pyridoxine treats isoniazid toxicity.
- Deferoxamine | Deferoxamine chelates iron.
= Fomepizole blocks alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing conversion of methanol or ethylene glycol to toxic acids.

? A patient poisoned with an organophosphate is receiving atropine. Which finding tells the nurse that enough atropine has been given?
+ Bronchial secretions have dried and breath sounds are clear
- The pupils have dilated to 6 mm | Pupil size is an unreliable endpoint.
- The heart rate has reached 120 per minute and stayed at that level | Tachycardia is not the titration target.
- The patient has become sleepy | Sedation is not a goal of therapy.
= Atropine is titrated to drying of pulmonary secretions and easier ventilation; pralidoxime is given to regenerate cholinesterase.

? A 2-year-old swallowed two of a grandparent's glipizide tablets. Glucose falls to 38 mg/dL again an hour after a dextrose bolus. Which medication should the nurse anticipate?
+ Octreotide
- Glucagon as the sole ongoing therapy | Glucagon is short acting and depends on glycogen stores.
- Regular insulin | Insulin would worsen hypoglycemia.
- Activated charcoal only | Charcoal does not correct established hypoglycemia.
= Octreotide suppresses the insulin release driven by sulfonylureas and prevents rebound hypoglycemia after dextrose.

? After a topical benzocaine spray, a patient is cyanotic with SpO2 85% that does not improve on 100% oxygen, and the arterial blood looks chocolate brown. Which treatment is indicated?
+ Methylene blue
- Hydroxocobalamin | That treats cyanide poisoning.
- Naloxone | Naloxone does not reduce methemoglobin.
- Sodium bicarbonate | Bicarbonate does not restore functional hemoglobin.
= Methemoglobinemia causes cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen with chocolate-coloured blood; methylene blue reduces methemoglobin.

@ 2.2
? A 17-year-old took about 20 g of acetaminophen 90 minutes ago. When should the serum level be drawn to guide treatment?
+ Four hours after the ingestion
- Immediately on arrival | A level before four hours cannot be plotted on the nomogram.
- Twelve hours after the ingestion | Waiting that long delays antidote beyond its best window.
- Only if liver enzymes rise | Enzymes rise after injury that treatment is meant to prevent.
= The Rumack-Matthew nomogram starts at four hours after a single acute ingestion, when absorption is complete.

? A patient's acetaminophen level drawn 4 hours after an acute ingestion is 190 mcg/mL. What does the nurse anticipate?
+ Starting N-acetylcysteine
- Discharge with reassurance | The level is above the treatment line at four hours.
- Repeating the level in 24 hours first | Delaying treatment allows hepatic injury.
- Hemodialysis | Dialysis is not routine treatment for acetaminophen.
= A four-hour level above 150 mcg/mL falls above the treatment line and warrants N-acetylcysteine.

? Why is the timing of N-acetylcysteine critical after acetaminophen overdose?
+ It is nearly fully protective when started within 8 hours
- It works only after liver enzymes have risen | It is most effective before injury occurs.
- It must be delayed until the drug is fully metabolized | Delay permits toxic metabolite build-up.
- It is effective only in the first 60 minutes | Its window is far longer than one hour.
= N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione; hepatotoxicity is rare when it begins within eight hours of ingestion.

? Twelve hours after a large acetaminophen overdose a patient feels well and asks to go home. What should the nurse tell her?
+ Early symptoms are mild or absent even when serious liver injury is developing
- Feeling well means the dose was not toxic | The first 24 hours are often asymptomatic.
- She can leave now as long as she drinks plenty of water and rests at home tomorrow | Hydration does not prevent hepatic necrosis.
- The danger passes after eight hours | Liver injury peaks at three to four days.
= Stage I acetaminophen toxicity is clinically quiet; liver enzymes rise at 24-72 hours and injury peaks at 72-96 hours.

? During the loading dose of IV N-acetylcysteine a patient develops flushing, hives and itching. BP and breathing are normal. What is the appropriate action?
+ Pause the infusion, give an antihistamine and restart at a slower rate
- Discontinue the antidote permanently | The reaction is rate related and treatment remains essential.
- Continue at the same rate and observe | The reaction is likely to worsen without intervention.
- Give IM epinephrine and stop all antidote treatment for the rest of the stay | Epinephrine is for airway or circulatory compromise.
= Anaphylactoid reactions to IV N-acetylcysteine are rate related and are managed by slowing the infusion and treating symptoms.

? Which laboratory results best track developing hepatotoxicity after acetaminophen overdose?
+ AST, ALT and INR
- Amylase and lipase | These reflect pancreatic injury.
- Troponin and BNP | These reflect cardiac injury and strain.
- Hemoglobin and platelet count | These do not reflect hepatocellular injury.
= Rising transaminases and a prolonged INR signal hepatocellular necrosis and failing synthetic function.

? A patient reports taking extra-strength acetaminophen "every couple of hours" for three days for a toothache. How does this change the assessment?
+ The nomogram cannot be used; treatment is guided by the level and liver enzymes
- The four-hour nomogram applies from the last dose | The nomogram is valid only for a single acute ingestion.
- No testing is needed because the doses were spread out | Repeated supratherapeutic dosing causes hepatotoxicity.
- A urine drug screen will show whether the total dose she has taken was a toxic one | Urine screens do not measure acetaminophen toxicity.
= Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion falls outside the nomogram; any detectable level with raised transaminases prompts treatment.

? A 22-year-old has ringing in the ears, vomiting, RR 32 and temperature 38.6 C after an intentional overdose. Arterial pH is 7.48 with a low bicarbonate. Which substance is most likely?
+ Aspirin
- Acetaminophen | Early acetaminophen toxicity is quiet without tinnitus or hyperpnea.
- Diphenhydramine | Anticholinergic poisoning does not cause tinnitus or this acid-base picture.
- Lorazepam | Benzodiazepines slow respiration.
= Salicylates stimulate the respiratory centre and uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, giving tinnitus, hyperpnea, fever and a mixed acid-base disorder.

? A patient with a salicylate level of 62 mg/dL is started on a sodium bicarbonate infusion. Which electrolyte must be kept normal for the therapy to work?
+ Potassium
- Chloride | Chloride does not determine urinary alkalinization.
- Phosphate | Phosphate does not determine urinary alkalinization.
- Sodium | Sodium is supplied by the infusion itself.
= Hypokalemia makes the kidney retain potassium in exchange for hydrogen ions, preventing the urine from becoming alkaline.

? Which finding in a salicylate-poisoned patient is an indication for emergent hemodialysis?
+ New confusion with pulmonary edema
- Mild tinnitus | Tinnitus alone is an early toxic effect.
- Nausea controlled with an antiemetic | Controlled nausea does not indicate severe poisoning.
- A respiratory rate of 24 | Mild hyperpnea is expected.
= Altered mental status, pulmonary or cerebral edema, renal failure, severe acidosis or very high levels call for dialysis.

? A severely salicylate-poisoned patient is breathing 36 times a minute. The team is considering sedation and intubation. What is the major risk?
+ Loss of compensatory hyperventilation causing fatal acidosis
- Vocal cord injury from salicylate | Salicylates do not injure the vocal cords.
- Malignant hyperthermia triggered by the sedative used for induction | That is unrelated to salicylates.
- Rebound hypertension | Hypertension is not the concern.
= Hyperventilation keeps the pH up; if ventilation falls after intubation, acidemia drives more salicylate into the brain.
