# Domain 3 — Trauma & Multisystem Shock (topics 3-5)

@ 3.3
? A restrained driver has BP 78/44 and HR 132 after two units of blood. The bedside ultrasound shows free fluid in the right upper quadrant. What is the next step?
+ Immediate transfer to the operating room for laparotomy
- CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast | An unstable patient should not go to the scanner.
- Admission for serial abdominal examinations | Observation is for stable patients.
- Diagnostic peritoneal lavage to confirm | The positive ultrasound already answers the question.
= A hypotensive patient with a positive FAST has intra-abdominal hemorrhage and needs operative control.

? A cyclist who fell onto the handlebars reports left upper abdominal pain and pain at the tip of the left shoulder. Which organ is most likely injured?
+ Spleen
- Liver | Liver injury refers pain to the right shoulder.
- Appendix | Appendiceal pain is right lower quadrant and not traumatic.
- Bladder | Bladder injury causes suprapubic pain and hematuria.
= Blood irritating the left hemidiaphragm refers pain to the left shoulder, known as Kehr's sign.

? A passenger has a band of bruising across the lower abdomen from a lap belt. The first CT is normal and he is stable. Why is he admitted for observation?
+ Bowel and mesenteric injuries may not appear on early imaging
- Bruising always indicates a splenic rupture | The spleen is not the organ typically injured by a lap belt.
- The bruise will need surgical drainage | Abdominal wall bruising is not drained.
- He requires prophylactic antibiotics | Antibiotics are not given for a bruise.
= A seat belt sign is associated with hollow viscus injury and lumbar fractures that can declare themselves hours later.

? A pedestrian struck by a truck has an unstable pelvis and BP 82/50. Which intervention should the nurse apply?
+ A pelvic binder centred over the greater trochanters
- A binder wrapped around the upper abdomen | Placement above the pelvis does not close the pelvic ring.
- Traction splints on both legs | Traction does not stabilize the pelvis and may worsen it.
- Military anti-shock trousers inflated fully | These are no longer recommended.
= A circumferential binder at the level of the greater trochanters reduces pelvic volume and tamponades venous bleeding.

? A colleague prepares to "rock" the pelvis again to recheck stability in a patient already found to have an unstable pelvic fracture. What should the nurse say?
+ "Repeated manipulation can dislodge clot and increase bleeding."
- "Go ahead; rechecking every hour is standard." | Repeated testing is harmful once instability is known.
- "Push harder so we can feel the crepitus." | Greater force increases hemorrhage.
- "Only rock it after the binder comes off." | The binder stays on and the pelvis is not re-tested.
= The pelvis is assessed gently once; further movement disrupts clot and can restart life-threatening bleeding.

? A patient has loops of bowel protruding through a knife wound. How should the nurse manage the wound?
+ Cover the bowel with sterile saline-moistened dressings
- Gently push the bowel back inside | Replacing bowel risks contamination and strangulation.
- Cover it with dry gauze and tape tightly | Dry dressings adhere to and desiccate bowel.
- Pour povidone-iodine over the bowel | Antiseptics damage the serosa.
= Eviscerated organs are kept moist and covered and are not reduced outside the operating room.

? A construction worker arrives with a length of rebar through the upper abdomen. He is awake with stable vital signs. What is the correct management of the object?
+ Stabilize it in place with bulky dressings
- Remove it promptly to control bleeding | The object may be tamponading vessels.
- Twist it gently to check how deep it lies | Movement causes further injury.
- Shorten it by bending it at the skin | Bending transmits force to the tissues.
= Impaled objects are secured and removed only in the operating room where bleeding can be controlled.

? A patient has fractures of the right ninth to eleventh ribs and right upper quadrant tenderness after a fall. Which injury should the nurse anticipate?
+ Liver laceration
- Splenic rupture | The spleen lies under the left lower ribs.
- Pancreatic transection | That follows mid-epigastric impact.
- Sigmoid perforation | The sigmoid is in the left lower quadrant.
= Lower right rib fractures are associated with injury to the underlying liver.

? Twelve hours after a fall, a patient develops bruising over both flanks. What does this finding suggest?
+ Retroperitoneal bleeding
- Superficial abrasion from the fall | Delayed flank bruising has a deeper source.
- An allergic reaction to contrast | Contrast reactions cause hives, not flank ecchymosis.
- Liver failure | Hepatic failure does not cause this localized pattern.
= Grey Turner's sign, flank ecchymosis, appears hours after retroperitoneal hemorrhage.

? A patient with a pelvic fracture has suprapubic pain, cannot void and has gross hematuria after catheter placement. Which study should the nurse anticipate?
+ A retrograde cystogram
- An intravenous pyelogram alone | It poorly evaluates the bladder.
- A plain abdominal film alone | Plain films cannot show a bladder tear.
- A scrotal ultrasound | It does not evaluate the bladder.
= Gross hematuria with a pelvic fracture suggests bladder rupture, which is confirmed by a cystogram.

@ 3.4
? Six hours after a cast was applied for a tibial fracture, a patient reports severe, deep pain that opioids do not relieve and that worsens when the toes are passively extended. What is the nurse's interpretation?
+ Developing compartment syndrome
- Normal fracture pain | Pain out of proportion that worsens with passive stretch is a warning sign.
- Deep vein thrombosis | Thrombosis does not cause pain on passive stretch this early.
- Opioid tolerance | Tolerance does not explain stretch pain.
= Pain out of proportion to the injury and pain with passive stretch are the earliest signs of compartment syndrome.

? For a patient with suspected compartment syndrome in a casted leg, which action should the nurse take while awaiting the surgeon?
+ Have the cast split and keep the limb at heart level
- Elevate the leg high on several pillows | Elevation lowers arterial inflow to tissue already under pressure.
- Apply ice packs around the cast | Cold causes vasoconstriction and worsens ischemia.
- Give more opioid and recheck in two hours | Waiting risks irreversible muscle and nerve death.
= Constriction is released and the limb is kept level to preserve perfusion pressure until fasciotomy.

? A nurse says compartment syndrome is unlikely because the pedal pulse is still present. Why is this incorrect?
+ Loss of the pulse is a late finding that appears after tissue damage
- A pulse is never present distal to a fracture | Pulses are usually present in an uncomplicated fracture.
- The pulse is an early and reliable sign | Arterial flow persists until compartment pressure is very high.
- Pulses cannot be felt through swelling | Swelling does not make this assessment meaningless.
= Compartment pressures compromise capillary perfusion long before they occlude arteries.

? A patient has an open tibial fracture with bone visible. What should the nurse do first after hemorrhage is controlled?
+ Cover the wound with a sterile saline dressing and prepare IV antibiotics
- Push the bone end back under the skin | Reduction outside surgery drags contamination into the wound.
- Scrub the bone with antiseptic solution | Antiseptics damage tissue and do not replace surgical debridement.
- Leave the wound uncovered for inspection | An open wound needs protection from further contamination.
= Open fractures are covered, splinted and given early IV antibiotics and tetanus prophylaxis before operative debridement.

? A patient has an isolated closed mid-shaft femur fracture with a shortened, painful thigh. Which device is appropriate?
+ A traction splint
- A sling and swathe | Those immobilize the upper limb.
- A knee immobilizer alone | It does not control femoral shaft displacement.
- A pelvic binder | A binder stabilizes the pelvic ring, not the femoral shaft.
= Traction splinting realigns a mid-shaft femur fracture, relieving pain and limiting bleeding into the thigh.

? Two days after a femur fracture, a patient becomes confused and short of breath with tiny red spots across the chest and axillae. What does the nurse suspect?
+ Fat embolism syndrome
- Opioid overdose | Opioids do not cause a petechial rash.
- Pneumonia | Pneumonia does not cause petechiae and confusion together.
- Allergic reaction | Allergy causes hives, not petechiae.
= Hypoxemia, neurologic change and petechiae 24-72 hours after a long-bone fracture form the classic triad of fat embolism.

? A patient arrives with an amputated thumb carried in a cup. How should the nurse care for the amputated part?
+ Wrap it in saline-moistened gauze, seal it in a bag and place the bag on ice water
- Place it directly on ice | Direct contact freezes and destroys the tissue.
- Soak it in a container of sterile water | Soaking macerates the tissue.
- Keep it warm under a blanket | Warmth shortens the time the part stays viable.
= The part is kept cool but not frozen and not soaked, to preserve it for possible replantation.

? A front-seat passenger whose knees struck the dashboard has a shortened, internally rotated, adducted right leg. Why is prompt reduction essential?
+ To reduce the risk of avascular necrosis of the femoral head
- To prevent leg-length growth disturbance | Growth plates are not the adult concern.
- To prevent a fat embolism | Dislocation does not cause fat embolism.
- To allow immediate weight bearing | Early weight bearing is not the goal.
= A posterior hip dislocation threatens the blood supply to the femoral head, and delay beyond about six hours raises the risk of necrosis.

? A patient's knee dislocation reduced spontaneously before arrival. The foot is warm with palpable pulses. Which assessment is still essential?
+ Ankle-brachial index and serial vascular checks
- A single pulse check, then discharge | Popliteal injury can be present despite palpable pulses.
- Range-of-motion testing only | Motion testing does not assess the artery.
- A straight-leg raise test | That evaluates nerve root irritation.
= Knee dislocation carries a high rate of popliteal artery injury, which may present late with intimal tears.

? A man is freed after his legs were trapped under a collapsed wall for six hours. Which complication should the nurse anticipate?
+ Hyperkalemia and myoglobin-induced kidney injury
- Hypokalemia and polyuria | Muscle breakdown releases potassium, and urine output falls.
- Hypercalcemia and constipation | Calcium usually falls early as it deposits in injured muscle.
- Metabolic alkalosis | Crush injury produces acidosis.
= Reperfusion of crushed muscle releases potassium and myoglobin, causing dysrhythmias and renal failure; aggressive fluids are started early.

@ 3.5
? A gardener has a deep laceration contaminated with soil. His last tetanus booster was seven years ago and he completed the primary series. What does he need?
+ A tetanus toxoid-containing booster
- Nothing, because he is covered for ten years | Dirty wounds need a booster if more than five years have passed.
- Tetanus immune globulin only | Immune globulin is for those without a complete primary series.
- Both booster and immune globulin | Immune globulin is unnecessary with a complete primary series.
= For contaminated wounds a booster is given when the last dose was more than five years ago; for clean minor wounds the interval is ten years.

? A patient with a puncture wound from a rusty nail has never been vaccinated against tetanus. What should the nurse anticipate?
+ Tetanus immune globulin and the first dose of vaccine at separate sites
- Vaccine alone | Vaccine takes weeks to protect an unimmunized person.
- Immune globulin alone | Passive protection is short lived and active immunity is still needed.
- Oral antibiotics alone | Antibiotics do not neutralize tetanus toxin.
= Unimmunized patients with tetanus-prone wounds receive passive protection with immune globulin plus active immunization.

? Which intervention does the most to prevent infection in a contaminated laceration?
+ High-volume irrigation under pressure
- Soaking the wound in povidone-iodine | Soaking does not remove debris and antiseptics damage tissue.
- Pouring hydrogen peroxide into the wound | Peroxide is toxic to healing tissue.
- Applying antibiotic ointment before closure | Ointment does not remove bacteria or debris.
= Mechanical irrigation with saline or clean water removes bacteria and foreign material and is the key step in wound care.

? During infiltration of lidocaine for a large laceration repair, the patient reports numbness around the mouth, ringing in the ears and a metallic taste. What should the nurse do?
+ Ask the provider to stop the injection and prepare for possible seizures
- Reassure the patient that this is normal | These are early signs of systemic toxicity.
- Encourage slow deep breathing only | Breathing techniques do not treat toxicity.
- Offer a sip of water for the taste | That ignores an evolving emergency.
= Perioral numbness, tinnitus and a metallic taste precede seizures and cardiac collapse in local anaesthetic toxicity; lipid emulsion is the antidote.

? Which wound is the best candidate for delayed rather than immediate closure?
+ A deep puncture wound to the foot sustained 14 hours ago
- A clean facial laceration from glass two hours ago | Fresh clean facial wounds are closed primarily.
- A clean kitchen-knife cut to the forearm one hour ago | A fresh clean wound is closed primarily.
- A shallow scalp laceration three hours old | Scalp wounds heal well with primary closure.
= Old, contaminated or puncture wounds carry a high infection risk and are left open or closed later.

? A painter accidentally injected his fingertip with a high-pressure paint sprayer. There is only a pinpoint wound and mild discomfort. What is the priority?
+ Emergent hand surgery consultation
- Cleaning the wound and discharge | The small entry wound hides extensive deep injury.
- A digital block and warm soaks | These delay definitive care and soaks may spread the material.
- Oral antibiotics and next-week follow-up | Delay leads to tissue necrosis and amputation.
= High-pressure injection drives material along tendon sheaths; early surgical debridement is needed despite a benign appearance.

? A man has a small laceration over the knuckle of his dominant hand after punching someone in the mouth. How should this wound be managed?
+ Irrigate thoroughly, leave it open and give antibiotics
- Close it with tissue adhesive | Sealing a human bite traps virulent bacteria.
- Suture it tightly to protect the joint | Closure markedly raises infection risk.
- Apply a bandage and no other treatment | These wounds frequently involve the joint and become infected.
= A clenched-fist injury is a human bite that often enters the joint; it is explored, irrigated, left open and treated with antibiotics.

? A machine has stripped the skin from the back of a worker's hand, leaving a flap attached at one edge. How should the nurse handle the flap?
+ Rinse gently, lay it back in position and cover with a moist saline dressing
- Trim the flap off to tidy the wound | Viable skin may be needed for coverage.
- Leave the flap hanging and apply dry gauze | Tension and drying kill the flap.
- Apply a tight elastic bandage | Pressure compromises the flap's blood supply.
= Avulsed or degloved tissue is preserved, kept moist and protected until a surgeon decides on its viability.

? A patient with diabetes has a leg wound with rapidly spreading redness, pain far out of proportion, grey blisters and a crackling feel under the skin. Temperature is 39.4 C and HR 126. What is needed?
+ Urgent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum IV antibiotics
- Oral antibiotics and a recheck in 48 hours | The infection will progress within hours.
- Warm compresses and limb elevation | Local measures do not halt necrosis.
- A topical antifungal cream | The infection is deep and bacterial.
= Pain out of proportion, crepitus, bullae and systemic toxicity indicate necrotizing soft tissue infection, a surgical emergency.

? A child had a cheek laceration sutured today. When should the nurse tell the parents to return for suture removal?
+ In three to five days
- In ten to fourteen days | That interval is for joints and the back and causes facial scarring.
- In three weeks | Sutures left this long leave track marks.
- Only if the sutures do not fall out | Non-absorbable sutures must be removed.
= Facial sutures are removed early, at about three to five days, to minimize scarring.
