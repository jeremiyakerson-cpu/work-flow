# Domain 9 — Mental Health Emergencies (topics 3-5)

@ 9.3
? A 29-year-old has sudden palpitations, chest tightness, tingling hands and a feeling she is about to die. Symptoms peaked within ten minutes. What must the nurse do before attributing this to a panic attack?
+ Obtain vital signs and an ECG to exclude a medical cause
- Tell her it is only anxiety | Cardiac and pulmonary causes present the same way.
- Give a sedative immediately | Sedation before assessment can mask a serious illness.
- Send her to the waiting room to calm down | Chest symptoms require prompt evaluation.
= Panic is a diagnosis of exclusion; dysrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, thyroid disease and stimulant use must be considered.

? Which nursing approach helps most during a panic attack?
+ Stay with the patient and give short, calm directions
- Leave the patient alone to settle | Being left alone increases fear.
- Give a detailed explanation of anxiety disorders | A person in panic cannot process complex information.
- Ask the patient to describe childhood events | Exploration is inappropriate during the acute episode.
= A calm presence, simple statements and coached slow breathing help the patient regain control.

? A hyperventilating patient has numbness around the mouth and cramped, flexed fingers. Which blood gas disturbance explains this?
+ Respiratory alkalosis
- Respiratory acidosis | Rapid breathing lowers, not raises, carbon dioxide.
- Metabolic acidosis | The cause is ventilatory.
- Metabolic alkalosis | The cause is ventilatory.
= Blowing off carbon dioxide raises the pH and lowers ionized calcium, causing tingling and carpopedal spasm.

? A 58-year-old with no history of anxiety has new "panic attacks" with weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor and a resting heart rate of 118. Which test is most relevant?
+ Thyroid-stimulating hormone
- A hemoglobin A1c | It does not explain the picture.
- A lipid panel | It does not explain the picture.
- A vitamin B12 level | It does not explain the picture.
= New anxiety in mid-life with weight loss, heat intolerance and tachycardia suggests hyperthyroidism.

? A patient is given a short supply of lorazepam for panic disorder. Which teaching point is most important?
+ It is for short-term use because of the risk of dependence
- It should be taken daily for life | Long-term use leads to tolerance and dependence.
- It is safe to combine with alcohol | The combination causes dangerous sedation.
- It can be stopped abruptly after months of use | Abrupt withdrawal can cause seizures.
= Benzodiazepines relieve acute anxiety, but long-term management relies on an SSRI and psychotherapy.

? A patient in a severe anxiety state is about to be discharged. When should the nurse give detailed medication teaching?
+ After the anxiety has decreased, with written instructions as well
- Immediately, while the patient is most anxious | Severe anxiety narrows attention and blocks learning.
- Never, as the patient cannot learn | Teaching is possible once anxiety falls.
- Only to a relative | The patient should be included when able.
= At severe and panic levels of anxiety the perceptual field narrows; teaching waits until the patient can attend.

? A combat veteran in the ED startles violently and shouts when a tray is dropped nearby. He appears to be reliving an event. What should the nurse do?
+ Speak calmly from in front of him, tell him where he is and that he is safe
- Grab his arms to steady him | Touch during a flashback may be perceived as an attack.
- Approach quietly from behind | An unseen approach increases the threat.
- Tell him sharply to stop | Commands escalate fear.
= During a flashback the nurse reorients gently, avoids sudden movement and touch, and keeps the environment quiet.

? A college student has palpitations, tremor and anxiety during exam week. Which question is most useful?
+ "How many energy drinks or caffeine tablets have you had?"
- "Do you prefer morning or evening classes?" | It does not identify a cause.
- "What is your favourite subject?" | It is irrelevant.
- "Do you live on campus?" | It does not identify a cause.
= Stimulants such as caffeine and decongestants are common, reversible causes of anxiety symptoms.

? Two hours after surviving a fatal car crash, an uninjured passenger is trembling, tearful and says she feels numb. What is the most appropriate nursing response?
+ Provide a quiet space, stay with her and explain that these reactions are common
- Tell her she is lucky to be alive | That dismisses her distress.
- Press her to describe the crash in detail | Forced recounting can worsen distress.
- Refer her straight to long-term psychiatric care | An acute stress reaction usually resolves with support.
= Early support focuses on safety, calm, connection and practical help rather than detailed debriefing.

? A patient starting an SSRI for panic disorder asks when it will work. What should the nurse say?
+ "It usually takes several weeks, and you may feel a little more jittery at first."
- "You will feel better within an hour." | SSRIs do not act immediately.
- "Stop it if you don't feel better in two days." | Early discontinuation prevents benefit.
- "Double the dose if you still feel anxious tomorrow." | Self-adjustment is unsafe.
= SSRIs take two to six weeks to work and can cause early activation; adherence and follow-up are stressed.

? A 74-year-old with COPD becomes newly anxious and shaky after her medications were changed. Which medication is the most likely cause?
+ A higher dose of albuterol with a new course of prednisone
- A daily multivitamin | It does not cause anxiety.
- A stool softener | It does not cause anxiety.
- Acetaminophen | It does not cause anxiety.
= Beta agonists and corticosteroids commonly cause tremor, palpitations and anxiety, especially in older adults.

@ 9.4
? A 79-year-old widower presents repeatedly with fatigue, poor appetite, weight loss and insomnia. Tests are normal. He says, "I'm just no use to anyone." What should the nurse consider?
+ Depression, with assessment of suicide risk
- Normal ageing | Persistent low mood and worthlessness are not normal ageing.
- Dementia | His memory is not the main problem.
- Malingering | The symptoms are genuine.
= Older adults often present with physical complaints; older men have the highest suicide rate.

? A 19-year-old began an SSRI two weeks ago. What should the nurse tell her parents to watch for?
+ New or worsening suicidal thoughts or agitation
- Hair loss | It is not a typical early effect.
- Loss of hearing | It is not an effect of SSRIs.
- Yellowing of the nails | It is not an effect of SSRIs.
= Antidepressants carry a warning about increased suicidal thinking in people under 25 early in treatment.

? A patient taking sertraline was given tramadol yesterday. He is agitated and sweating, with a temperature of 39.2 C, tremor and rhythmic jerking at the ankles. What should the nurse suspect?
+ Serotonin syndrome
- Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | That follows antipsychotics and develops over days with rigidity rather than clonus.
- Opioid withdrawal | Withdrawal does not cause clonus and high fever.
- Heat exhaustion | The drug combination and clonus point elsewhere.
= Combining serotonergic drugs causes agitation, autonomic instability and clonus, usually within hours.

? A patient taking phenelzine develops a pounding headache and BP 226/124 after a meal of aged cheese and cured sausage. What caused this?
+ A reaction between the MAO inhibitor and tyramine in the food
- An allergy to dairy | Allergy does not cause severe hypertension.
- Food poisoning | That causes vomiting and diarrhea.
- A panic attack | Panic does not raise blood pressure to this degree.
= MAO inhibitors block the breakdown of tyramine, which releases norepinephrine and causes hypertensive crisis.

? A patient has not slept for four days. He talks rapidly, jumps between topics and says he has bought three cars because he is "about to be a billionaire." Which nursing intervention is appropriate?
+ Place him in a low-stimulation area and set calm, consistent limits
- Encourage him to join a group discussion | Groups overstimulate a manic patient.
- Debate his business plans with him | Arguing escalates the mania.
- Give him a stimulating puzzle | Stimulation worsens symptoms.
= Mania is managed with a quiet environment, clear limits, attention to safety, fluids and food he can eat on the move.

? A patient on lithium has had vomiting and diarrhea for three days. He now has a coarse tremor, unsteady gait and confusion. What should the nurse suspect?
+ Lithium toxicity
- Alcohol intoxication | There is no history of drinking.
- Worsening mania | These are neurologic signs, not mood symptoms.
- Viral labyrinthitis | That does not cause tremor and confusion.
= Dehydration reduces lithium excretion; coarse tremor, ataxia and confusion indicate toxicity.

? Which treatment should the nurse anticipate for significant lithium toxicity?
+ IV normal saline, with hemodialysis if severe
- Activated charcoal | Lithium is not adsorbed by charcoal.
- A thiazide diuretic | Thiazides raise lithium levels.
- An NSAID for the tremor | NSAIDs raise lithium levels.
= Saline promotes lithium excretion; dialysis is used for high levels, severe symptoms or kidney impairment.

? A patient with bipolar disorder was started on an antidepressant alone two weeks ago. He is now euphoric, sleeps two hours a night and is spending recklessly. What has happened?
+ The antidepressant has triggered a manic episode
- The depression has been cured | This is a switch into mania, not recovery.
- He has developed an allergy | These are not allergic symptoms.
- The dose is too low | A higher dose would worsen the mania.
= Antidepressants without a mood stabilizer can precipitate mania in bipolar disorder.

? A severely depressed patient began treatment ten days ago and now has more energy, although his mood is still very low. Why is this a period of increased concern?
+ He may now have the energy to act on suicidal thoughts
- He is developing mania | Low mood with more energy is not mania.
- The medication has stopped working | Energy returning is an early effect.
- He no longer needs observation | Observation should be closer, not less.
= Energy and motivation often improve before mood, raising suicide risk in the early weeks of treatment.

? A patient took an intentional overdose of bupropion. Which complication should the nurse prepare for?
+ Seizures
- Hypoglycemia | That is not the characteristic effect.
- Bradycardia with heart block | Tachycardia is more typical.
- Respiratory depression alone | Seizures are the main danger.
= Bupropion lowers the seizure threshold, and overdose commonly causes seizures and dysrhythmias.

? A patient with a mood disorder lies motionless and mute, stares fixedly, and holds any posture in which her arm is placed. Which medication is first-line?
+ A benzodiazepine such as lorazepam
- A high-potency antipsychotic | Antipsychotics can worsen this condition.
- A stimulant | It is not the treatment.
- An opioid | It is not the treatment.
= Immobility, mutism and waxy flexibility indicate catatonia, which often responds dramatically to lorazepam.

@ 9.5
? A man who drinks a litre of vodka daily had his last drink 60 hours ago. He is confused, sees snakes on the floor, and has a temperature of 38.7 C, HR 142 and BP 188/104. What is this condition?
+ Delirium tremens
- Alcohol intoxication | Intoxication occurs with drinking, not three days after stopping.
- Wernicke encephalopathy | That causes confusion with ataxia and eye signs, not autonomic storm.
- Hepatic encephalopathy | That does not cause this degree of hyperadrenergic activity.
= Delirium tremens develops 48-96 hours after the last drink with delirium, hallucinations and autonomic instability.

? Which medication class is first-line for alcohol withdrawal?
+ Benzodiazepines
- Antipsychotics alone | They lower the seizure threshold and do not prevent delirium tremens.
- Opioids | They do not treat alcohol withdrawal.
- Beta blockers alone | They hide signs without preventing seizures.
= Benzodiazepines substitute for alcohol at the GABA receptor, preventing seizures and delirium tremens.

? What is the purpose of a withdrawal scale such as the CIWA-Ar?
+ To match benzodiazepine dosing to the severity of symptoms
- To measure the blood alcohol level | The scale is a clinical assessment.
- To diagnose liver failure | It does not assess the liver.
- To decide who needs restraints | That is not its purpose.
= Symptom-triggered dosing guided by a validated scale gives enough medication without over-sedation.

? A patient with alcohol dependence had his last drink ten hours ago. Which symptom is expected first?
+ Tremor with anxiety and sweating
- Delirium with hallucinations | Delirium tremens appears two to four days later.
- Coma | Coma is not an early withdrawal sign.
- Slow heart rate | Withdrawal causes tachycardia.
= Minor withdrawal begins 6-12 hours after the last drink; seizures peak at 12-48 hours and delirium tremens later.

? An intoxicated man with a scalp wound was drowsy but rousable an hour ago. He is now difficult to wake. What should the nurse do?
+ Reassess immediately and notify the provider of the change
- Let him sleep it off | A falling level of consciousness must not be assumed to be alcohol.
- Give coffee | It does nothing for an intracranial injury.
- Dim the lights and close the door | That delays recognition of deterioration.
= Intoxication should improve with time; a declining level of consciousness suggests head injury or another cause.

? A patient who uses heroin daily has yawning, a runny nose, gooseflesh, dilated pupils, abdominal cramps and diarrhea. What is happening?
+ Opioid withdrawal
- Opioid overdose | Overdose causes pinpoint pupils and slow breathing.
- Alcohol withdrawal | That causes tremor and autonomic hyperactivity without gooseflesh and rhinorrhea.
- Stimulant intoxication | That causes agitation and hypertension.
= Opioid withdrawal is intensely uncomfortable but rarely life threatening; it is treated with buprenorphine or symptomatic care.

? A patient who injected fentanyl two hours ago asks for buprenorphine. He has no withdrawal signs yet. Why should the first dose be delayed?
+ Given too early, it can precipitate sudden severe withdrawal
- It has no effect on fentanyl users | It is effective when timed correctly.
- It will cause an overdose | Its partial agonist action makes overdose less likely.
- It must be given only in a clinic | It can be started in the ED.
= Buprenorphine displaces full agonists from the receptor; it is started once moderate withdrawal is present.

? A patient abruptly stopped taking high-dose alprazolam four days ago and has had a seizure. What is the appropriate treatment?
+ A benzodiazepine followed by a gradual taper
- Phenytoin alone | It is not effective for sedative withdrawal seizures.
- Naloxone | It does not treat sedative withdrawal.
- Observation without medication | Untreated withdrawal can be fatal.
= Abrupt benzodiazepine withdrawal causes seizures and delirium; the drug is restarted and slowly reduced.

? A patient who used methamphetamine is agitated and paranoid with a temperature of 39.8 C, HR 156 and BP 198/118. What should the nurse anticipate?
+ IV benzodiazepines and active cooling
- A beta blocker as the only treatment | Benzodiazepines are first-line for stimulant toxicity.
- Physical restraint alone | Struggling against restraints worsens hyperthermia.
- Haloperidol alone in large doses | It can lower the seizure threshold and impair heat loss.
= Sedation with benzodiazepines controls agitation, blood pressure and heat production in stimulant toxicity.

? A patient is ready for discharge after an opioid overdose reversed with naloxone. Which intervention has the greatest potential to prevent death?
+ Giving a take-home naloxone kit with teaching for the patient and family
- Warning the patient never to use again | Warnings alone do not prevent overdose.
- Withholding all follow-up to discourage return visits | That increases risk.
- Advising the patient to use alone | Using alone increases the risk of death.
= Naloxone distribution and linkage to treatment reduce deaths after non-fatal overdose.

? A patient with an alcohol-related injury says he has "thought about cutting down." Which nursing approach is most effective?
+ A brief, non-judgmental conversation linking his drinking to the injury, with referral
- A lecture on the dangers of alcohol | Lecturing provokes defensiveness.
- Avoiding the subject to prevent embarrassment | That misses a teachable moment.
- Telling him he is an alcoholic | Labelling damages rapport.
= Screening, brief intervention and referral in the ED reduce harmful drinking, especially when a patient shows readiness to change.
